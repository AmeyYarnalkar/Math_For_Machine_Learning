# Median for Probabilistic Distribution

## What does “median” mean at its root?

Before probability, before distributions, before math:

> **Median means “middle.”**

That’s it.

---

## Start from the simplest case (no probability)

Take raw data:

```
2, 4, 7, 10, 15
```

* Middle value = **7**
* Why?

  * 2 values on the left
  * 2 values on the right

So the median is the value that **splits the data into two equal halves**.

---

## Now introduce probability (why CDF appears)

In probability, we often:

* Don’t have actual data yet
* Only know **how likely values are**

So instead of counting **number of values**, we count **probability mass**.

This changes the question to:

> “At what value is half of the probability on the left and half on the right?”

That’s why **cumulative probability** matters.

---

## Why exactly 50%?

Because **50% is the definition of “half.”**

* Less than 50% → you’re still in the left half
* More than 50% → you’ve crossed into the right half

So the **median is where the crossing happens**.

Not because of convention.
Not because of formula.
Because of **meaning**.

---

## Why cumulative probability, not point probability?

Because probability:

* Does **not live at a point** (especially for continuous data)
* Lives in **accumulation**

You can’t ask:

> “Does this single value have 50% probability?”

That makes no sense.

You must ask:

> “Up to what value have I accumulated 50% of the probability?”

That’s exactly what the **CDF** measures.

---

## One-sentence logic chain 

> Median means middle →
> middle means half on each side →
> probability replaces counts →
> cumulative probability reaching 50% defines the median.

No gaps. No magic.

---

## Why “equal OR crossing” 50%?

Because of **discrete jumps**.

* Continuous distributions → smooth → hit exactly 0.5
* Discrete distributions → jump over 0.5

So we say:

> “The median is the value where cumulative probability reaches **or exceeds** 50%.”

This makes the definition work **everywhere**.
