You already learned **Bernoulli distribution**:

> One experiment
> Two outcomes
> Success (1) with probability **p**
> Failure (0) with probability **1 − p**

Example:

* Toss a coin **once**
* Head = success
* Tail = failure

That’s Bernoulli. **One trial only.**

---

## The obvious question (this leads to Binomial)

Now ask yourself this:

> What if I repeat the **same Bernoulli experiment multiple times**?

Example:

* Toss the coin **10 times**
* Each toss is:

  * independent
  * same probability **p**

Now you don’t care about **each toss individually**.
You care about:

> **How many heads did I get in total?**

⚠️ This question **creates** the Binomial distribution.

---

##  What Binomial Distribution REALLY is

> **Binomial = Sum of independent Bernoulli trials**

That’s the core idea. Nothing more.

If:

* You perform **n** independent Bernoulli trials
* Each trial has success probability **p**

Then:

* The number of successes **X** follows a **Binomial distribution**

We write:

```
X ~ Binomial(n, p)
```

---

## Simple example (no formula yet)

### Example: Coin toss

* Toss a fair coin **5 times**
* Success = Head
* p = 0.5
* n = 5

Possible outcomes for number of heads:

```
0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5
```

Binomial answers questions like:

* What is the probability of getting **exactly 3 heads**?
* What is the probability of getting **at least 1 head**?
* What is the probability of getting **no heads**?

Bernoulli **cannot** answer this.
Binomial **can**.

---

## 5️⃣ Why order does NOT matter (important intuition)

Suppose:

* 3 heads in 5 tosses

These are all valid:

```
HHHTT
HTHHT
THTHH
```

Binomial **does not care about order**
It only cares about:

> **how many successes**

That’s why combinations appear later.

---

## The Binomial Probability Formula (now it will make sense)

Probability of getting **exactly k successes**:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}
$$

Let me break this brutally simply:

### 1️⃣ $ p^k $

* Probability of getting **k successes**

### 2️⃣ $ (1 - p)^{n - k} $

* Probability of getting **n − k failures**

### 3️⃣ $ \binom{n}{k} $

* Number of ways to arrange those successes and failures
  (because order doesn’t matter)

That’s it. No mystery.

---

## Concrete numeric example

**Question**
Toss a fair coin 5 times.
What is the probability of getting **exactly 2 heads**?

Given:

* n = 5
* p = 0.5
* k = 2

$$
P(X=2) = \binom{5}{2}(0.5)^2(0.5)^3
$$

$$
= 10 \times 0.5^5 = 0.3125
$$

> **Binomial** = counting yes decisions over many tries

### Your next move (don’t stay comfortable)

Before moving forward,


## What nCk actually means 

**nCk = “How many different ways can I choose k items from n items,
when order does NOT matter?”**

---

## Start with a tiny, concrete example

Say you have **5 positions** (coin tosses):

```
1 2 3 4 5
```

You want **exactly 2 heads**.

So the question becomes:

> In how many ways can I choose **2 positions** out of these 5 to place Heads?

That count is **5C2**.

---

## Step 1: Count if order *did* matter (this is the trap)

If order mattered, you would count like this:

Choose 2 positions **in order**:

```
5 choices for first head
4 choices for second head
```

Total:

```
5 × 4 = 20
```

But this is **wrong** for Binomial.

Why?

Because:

```
(1,2) and (2,1)
```

are being counted separately — but they represent the **same outcome**
(Heads at positions 1 and 2).

So we **overcounted**.

---

## Step 2: Fix the overcounting (this is the key insight)

For every pair of positions, how many times did we count it?

Since we’re choosing **2 items**, the order can be rearranged in:

```
2! = 2 ways
```

So we divide by **2!**

$$
5C2 = \frac{5 × 4}{2!} = \frac{20}{2} = 10
$$

Boom. That’s it.

---

## Generalizing this logic 

If:

* You choose **k items**
* From **n items**

Then:

### Step A: Count ordered selections

$$
n × (n-1) × (n-2) × ... × (n-k+1)
$$

### Step B: Remove ordering duplicates

Each group is counted **k! times**, so divide by **k!**

Final formula:
$$
\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
$$
.
This is **count → overcount → correct**.

---

## One more example 

### Example: 5 tosses, exactly 3 heads

* Choose 3 positions out of 5

$$
5C3 = \frac{5!}{3!2!}
$$

$$
= \frac{5×4×3×2×1}{(3×2×1)(2×1)} = 10
$$

These correspond to patterns like:

```
HHHTT
HHTHT
HTHHT
TTHHH
```

Order of tosses doesn’t matter — only **which positions are heads**.

---

## Why nCk is unavoidable in Binomial

In Binomial probability:
$$
P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}
$$

* $p^k$: probability of **k successes**
* $(1-p)^{n-k}$: probability of **failures**
* **nCk**: number of ways those successes can be placed

If you remove nCk, you’re counting **only one arrangement**, not all.

---

## We are talking about **Binomial distribution**
$$
X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)
$$

---

## Mean (Expectation)

$$
\boxed{E[X] = np}
$$

**Why this makes sense**

* Binomial = sum of (n) Bernoulli trials
* Each Bernoulli has mean (p)
* Add them → (np)

---

## Variance

$$
\boxed{Var(X) = np(1-p)}
$$

**Why**

* Each Bernoulli has variance (p(1-p))
* Variances add for independent trials

---

## Standard Deviation

$$
\boxed{\sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)}}
$$

Just square-root of variance. 

---

## Mode (most probable value)

$$
\boxed{\text{Mode} = \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor}
$$

### Special case

If ((n+1)p) is an **integer**, there are **two modes**:
$$
(n+1)p - 1 \quad \text{and} \quad (n+1)p
$$

---

## Median (middle value)

No exact closed formula.

**Practical rule (what actually works):**
$$
\boxed{\text{Median} \approx np}
$$

More precisely:
$$
\text{Median} \in {\lfloor np \rfloor,; \lceil np \rceil}
$$

---

## One-line summary table (memorize this)

| Measure  | Formula                  |
| -------- | ------------------------ |
| Mean     | (np)                     |
| Variance | (np(1-p))                |
| Std Dev  | (\sqrt{np(1-p)})         |
| Mode     | (\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor) |
| Median   | (\approx np)             |

---

## Symmetric case (important)

If:
$$
p = 0.5
$$

Then:

```
Mean = Median = Mode = np
```

Perfectly balanced distribution.
