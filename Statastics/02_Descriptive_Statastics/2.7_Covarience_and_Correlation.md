

#  Covariance and Correlation

Covariance and correlation are statistical measures used to **understand the relationship between two variables**.

They answer:

> **When one variable changes, how does the other behave?**

---

## Covariance

### What is Covariance?

**Covariance measures how two variables change together** and tells the **direction** of their relationship.

Plain English:

> It tells whether two variables move in the **same direction**,
> **opposite direction**, or have **no linear relationship**.

---

### Interpretation

* **Positive covariance (+)**
  As (X) increases, (Y) also increases
  (or both decrease together)

* **Negative covariance (−)**
  As (X) increases, (Y) decreases

* **Zero covariance (≈ 0)**
  No linear relationship

 Covariance tells **direction**, not **strength**.

---

### Sample Covariance Formula

$$
\text{Cov}(X, Y) =
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{n - 1}
$$

* $x_i, y_i$ → data points
* $\bar{x}, \bar{y}$ → sample means
* $n$ → sample size

(Bessel’s correction applies here as well.)

---

### Advantages of Covariance

* Quantifies **joint variability**
* Identifies **direction of relationship**
* Fundamental building block for correlation

---

### Disadvantages of Covariance (Important)

* No fixed range
  [
  -\infty ; \text{to} ; +\infty
  ]
* Depends on **units of measurement**
* Cannot compare strength across datasets

👉 You can say:

* “Positive relationship” or “Negative relationship”
  But you **cannot say how strong it is**

---

## Why Covariance Is Not Enough

Because:

* No standard scale
* No comparability
* Hard to interpret magnitude

👉 To fix this, we use **Correlation**

---

## Correlation

Correlation measures **both direction and strength**
of the linear relationship between two variables.

---

## Pearson Correlation Coefficient

### Definition

Pearson correlation standardizes covariance
and restricts values between **−1 and +1**.

---

### Formula

$$
ρ(X, Y)  =
\frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \cdot \sigma_Y}
$$

For sample data:

$$
r =
\frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{s_X \cdot s_Y}
$$

* (s_X, s_Y) → standard deviations

---

### Interpretation

* **+1** → Perfect positive linear relationship
* **−1** → Perfect negative linear relationship
* **0** → No linear relationship

Strength:

* Closer to ±1 → stronger relationship
* Closer to 0 → weaker relationship

---

### Limitations of Pearson Correlation

* Assumes **linear relationship**
* Sensitive to **outliers**
* Can be misleading for non-linear data

👉 High correlation ≠ causation
👉 Zero correlation ≠ no relationship

---

## Spearman Rank Correlation

### Why Spearman?

Used when:

* Relationship is **non-linear**
* Data contains **outliers**
* Data is **ordinal**

---

### Core Idea

Instead of raw values:

* Convert data into **ranks**
* Then compute correlation on ranks

---

### What is Rank?

Rank is the **position of a value**
when the data is sorted.

Example:

* Smallest value → rank 1
* Largest value → rank n

---

### Formula

$$
r_s =
\frac{\text{Cov}(\text{rank}(X), \text{rank}(Y))}
{\sigma_{\text{rank}(X)} \cdot \sigma_{\text{rank}(Y)}}
$$

---

### Key Properties

* Captures **monotonic relationships**
* Robust to outliers
* Works even when Pearson fails

---

## Covariance vs Correlation (Mental Table)

| Aspect         | Covariance | Correlation |
| -------------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| Direction      | Yes        | Yes         |
| Strength       | No         | Yes         |
| Range          | −∞ to +∞   | −1 to +1    |
| Unit dependent | Yes        | No          |
| Comparability  | Poor       | Excellent   |
