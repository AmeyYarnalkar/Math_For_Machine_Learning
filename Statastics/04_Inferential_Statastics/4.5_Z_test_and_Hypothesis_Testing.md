
# Z-Test 

## Why Do We Need a Z-Test?

In real life, we often want to test a claim about a population.

Example:

* A company claims the average battery life is 10 hours.
* Is that really true?

We cannot test the entire population.
So we take a sample.

But sample means naturally vary.

So the real question becomes:

> Is the difference between our sample mean and the claimed mean large enough to doubt the claim?

To answer that, we need a standardized way to measure the difference.

That’s where Z-test comes in.

---

### Core Idea 

> A Z-test measures how many standard errors away the sample mean is from the hypothesized population mean.

It converts difference into a scale we can interpret using the normal distribution.

---

## When Do We Use a Z-Test?

We use Z-test when:

1. Population standard deviation (σ) is known.
2. Sample is random.
3. Population is normal OR sample size ≥ 30 (Central Limit Theorem).

Important:
Z-test assumes we know σ.
In real life, that’s rare — and that leads to t-test (next topic).

---

##  Hypothesis Setup

Before testing anything, we define two hypotheses.

### Null Hypothesis (H₀)

This is the claim we assume is true.

Example:
$$
H_0: \mu = 10
$$

### Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)

This challenges the null.

Three types:

* Two-tailed:
  $$
  H_1: \mu \ne 10
  $$

* Right-tailed:
  $$
  H_1: \mu > 10
  $$

* Left-tailed:
  $$
  H_1: \mu < 10
  $$

---

## Z-Test Statistic Formula

$$
Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}
$$

Where:

* $ \bar{X} $ = Sample mean
* $ \mu $ = Hypothesized population mean
* $ \sigma $ = Population standard deviation
* $ n $ = Sample size
* $ \sigma/\sqrt{n} $ = Standard Error

---

### Interpretation

If:

Z = 2

That means:

> The sample mean is 2 standard errors away from the null mean.

It tells us how unusual our sample result is.

---

## Step-by-Step Procedure

### Step 1: State H₀ and H₁

Define what you are testing.

---

### Step 2: Choose Significance Level (α)

Common values:

* 0.05
* 0.01

### What does α mean?

In simple words:

> α is the maximum probability we are willing to accept of rejecting a true null hypothesis.

It acts like a decision boundary.

If the result falls in the most extreme α% region of the distribution, we reject H₀.

Example:

If α = 0.05 (two-tailed),
we reject if result lies in extreme 5% (2.5% on each side).

So α defines how strict we are.

Smaller α → harder to reject.

---

### Step 3: Compute Z Statistic

Calculate using formula.

---

### Step 4: Find Critical Value or P-Value

Two methods:

### Method 1: Critical Value Method

For α = 0.05 (two-tailed):
Critical values ≈ ±1.96

If |Z| > 1.96 → Reject H₀

---

### Method 2: P-Value Method

Find probability of getting such extreme Z.

If:

p-value < α → Reject H₀

---

### Decision Rule (Using 68–95–99 Rule)

Within:

* ±1 SD → 68%
* ±2 SD → 95%
* ±3 SD → 99.7%

If Z is large (like 3), result is extremely rare under null.

Rare result → strong evidence against null.

---


## Simple Worked Example (Conceptual)

A company claims:

Average battery life = 10 hours.

We take a sample:

Sample mean = 10.8
σ = 1.5
n = 36
α = 0.05

Compute:

Standard error:
$$
SE = \frac{1.5}{\sqrt{36}} = 0.25
$$

Z:
$$
Z = \frac{10.8 - 10}{0.25} = 3.2
$$

Since 3.2 > 1.96 → Reject H₀.

Conclusion:

There is sufficient evidence at 5% level to reject the claim that average battery life is 10 hours.

---

# One-Sentence Definition 

> A Z-test checks whether the difference between a sample average and a claimed average is large enough to not be due to random chance.
