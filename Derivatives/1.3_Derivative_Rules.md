## Derivative Rules 

##  Constant Rule

### Rule

If a function is just a number:

$$
f(x)=c
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=0
$$

### Intuition

A constant doesn’t change when (x) changes → slope is zero.

### Example

$$
f(x)=10 \Rightarrow f'(x)=0
$$

**Mental picture:** flat horizontal line.

---

##  Constant Multiple Rule

### Rule

If a constant multiplies a function:

$$
f(x)=a \cdot g(x)
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=a \cdot g'(x)
$$

### Intuition

Scaling a function scales its slope equally.
If you stretch the graph vertically by 5 → slope also becomes 5×.

### Example

$$
f(x)=7x^3
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=7 \cdot 3x^2 = 21x^2
$$

👉 Constant stays outside. Just differentiate the inside part.

---

##  Sum Rule

### Rule

If two functions are added:

$$
f(x)=g(x)+h(x)
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=g'(x)+h'(x)
$$

### Intuition

Total change = change from part 1 + change from part 2.

Slope distributes over addition.

### Example

$$
f(x)=x^2+x^3
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=2x+3x^2
$$

No mixing. Differentiate each term separately.

---

##  Difference Rule

### Rule

If functions are subtracted:

$$
f(x)=g(x)-h(x)
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=g'(x)-h'(x)
$$

### Intuition

Same as sum rule — derivative respects subtraction.

### Example

$$
f(x)=x^4-x
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=4x^3-1
$$

---

##  Why All These Rules Exist 

Derivative measures **rate of change**.

* Adding functions → adds their rates.
* Subtracting → subtracts their rates.
* Multiplying by a constant → scales the rate.
* Constant alone → no change → zero.

Everything here is just logic about change — not magic formulas.

---

##  Quick  Checklist (Use This in Problems)

When you see a function:

1. Break into terms using + or −.
2. Pull constants outside.
3. Apply power rule on each term.
4. Combine.

Example:
$$
f(x)=5x^3 + 2x^2 - 7
$$

Step-wise thinking:

* Constant multiple → keep 5, keep 2
* Sum/difference → separate terms
* Constant → derivative 0

Final:
$$
f'(x)=15x^2 + 4x
$$

## Product Rule — When Functions Multiply

### Rule

If:

$$
f(x)=g(x)\cdot h(x)
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=g'(x)h(x)+g(x)h'(x)
$$

**Short memory trick:**\
👉 *Derivative of first × second* \
👉 *plus first × derivative of second*

---

### Intuition 

When two changing things multiply, the total change comes from **both** parts changing.

Think of:

* $g(x)$ changing while $h(x)$ stays
* $h(x)$ changing while $g(x)$ stays

Add both effects → that’s why two terms appear.

---

### Example

$$
f(x)=x^2 \cdot x^3
$$

Using product rule:

* (g=x^2,; h=x^3)
* (g'=2x,; h'=3x^2)

$$
f'(x)=2x(x^3)+x^2(3x^2)=2x^4+3x^4=5x^4
$$

(Yes, you could simplify first — but rule still works.)

---

### When You MUST Use Product Rule

If you see:

* $(x+1)(x^2+3)$
* $x^2 \sin x$
* Anything multiplied where both depend on (x)

Don’t try shortcuts — apply the rule.

---

## Quotient Rule — When Functions Divide

### Rule

If:

$$
f(x)=\frac{g(x)}{h(x)}
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=\frac{g'(x)h(x)-g(x)h'(x)}{h(x)^2}
$$

---

### Intuition

Division means:

* Numerator changes
* Denominator changes

But denominator changing affects the slope **inversely**, which is why subtraction appears and the denominator gets squared.

---

### Example

$$
f(x)=\frac{x^2}{x}
$$

Let:

* $g=x^2, g'=2x$
* $h=x,; h'=1$

$$
f'(x)=\frac{2x(x)-x^2(1)}{x^2}
=\frac{2x^2-x^2}{x^2}
=1
$$

Makes sense because original simplifies to (x).

---


## Quick Comparison (So It Sticks)

| Situation           | Rule Idea                         |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Add/Subtract        | Differentiate each term           |
| Constant × Function | Pull constant out                 |
| Multiply            | Two terms added                   |
| Divide              | Two terms subtracted, over denom² |
