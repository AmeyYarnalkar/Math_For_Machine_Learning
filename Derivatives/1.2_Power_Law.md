## Power Rule in Derivatives 

###  What the power rule says

If a function looks like:

$$
f(x) = x^n
$$

then its derivative is:

$$
f'(x) = n \cdot x^{,n-1}
$$

That’s it.
**Bring the power down → reduce the power by 1.**

---

###  Intuition (Why this works)

Derivative = *rate of change* or *slope*.

When you increase (x) a tiny bit:

* Higher powers grow faster.
* The exponent tells you how strongly the function reacts to change.

So:

* (x^1) → constant slope
* (x^2) → slope increases linearly
* (x^3) → slope increases even faster

The coefficient (n) captures that growth speed.

---

###  Basic Examples

#### Example 1

$$
f(x)=x^3
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=3x^2
$$

Meaning: slope at any point depends on (x^2).

---

#### Example 2

$$
f(x)=5x^4
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=20x^3
$$

Rule:

* Multiply by exponent.
* Subtract 1 from exponent.

---

#### Example 3 — Negative power

$$
f(x)=x^{-2}
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=-2x^{-3}
$$

Power rule works for **negative** powers too.

---

#### Example 4 — Fractional power

$$
f(x)=x^{1/2}
$$
Derivative:
$$
f'(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}
$$

Also works for roots.

---

###  Special Cases (Important)

#### Constant

$$
f(x)=7
$$
Derivative = **0**
(No change → no slope.)

#### Linear

$$
f(x)=x
$$
Derivative = **1**
Slope is constant.

---

###  General Form

If:

$$
f(x)=a x^n
$$

then:

$$
f'(x)=a n x^{n-1}
$$

Coefficient stays → exponent comes down → exponent reduces by 1.

---

### Quick  Algorithm

When you see (x^n):

1. Multiply by power.
2. Reduce power by 1.
3. Done.

No limit formulas. No heavy math. Just pattern.
