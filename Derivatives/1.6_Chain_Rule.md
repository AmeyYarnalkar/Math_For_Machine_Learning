## Chain Rule 

Chain rule is used when a function is **inside another function**.
Basically: derivative of a *composition*.

If you see **layers**, you need chain rule.

---

## Core Idea

If

$$
y = f(g(x))
$$

then

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)
$$

👉 **Derivative of outer function**\
👉 multiply by **derivative of inner function**

---

## Intuition 

Think of change happening in two stages:

1. (x) changes → inner function (g(x)) changes
2. That change affects outer function (f(\cdot))

Total change = outer change × inner change.

You’re adjusting for the fact that the inside is moving.

---

## Mechanical Steps 

When you see something like:

$$
(\text{something})^{power}, \quad \sin(\text{something}), \quad e^{(\text{something})}
$$

follow this:

### Step 1 — Identify **outer** and **inner**

Example:
$$
(3x^2+1)^5
$$

* Outer = (u^5)
* Inner = (u=3x^2+1)

---

### Step 2 — Differentiate the **outer** (keep inner same)

$$
5(3x^2+1)^4
$$

Don’t touch inside yet.

---

### Step 3 — Multiply by derivative of **inner**

Derivative of $3x^2+1$ is $6x$.

---

### Step 4 — Final Answer

$$
5(3x^2+1)^4 \cdot 6x
$$

## Example 1 — Power Function

$$
f(x)=(x^2+1)^3
$$

Outer derivative:
$$
3(x^2+1)^2
$$

Inner derivative:
$$
2x
$$

Final:
$$
f'(x)=6x(x^2+1)^2
$$

---

##  Example 2 — Trigonometric

$$
f(x)=\sin(3x)
$$

Outer derivative:
$$
\cos(3x)
$$

Inner derivative:
$$
3
$$

Final:
$$
f'(x)=3\cos(3x)
$$

👉 Without chain rule people wrongly write just (\cos(3x)).

---

## Example 3 — Exponential

$$
f(x)=e^{x^2}
$$

Outer derivative:
$$
e^{x^2}
$$

Inner derivative:
$$
2x
$$

Final:
$$
f'(x)=2x,e^{x^2}
$$

---

##  Example 4 — Logarithmic

$$
f(x)=\ln(x^3+1)
$$

Outer derivative:
$$
\frac{1}{x^3+1}
$$

Inner derivative:
$$
3x^2
$$

Final:
$$
f'(x)=\frac{3x^2}{x^3+1}
$$

## How to Recognize Chain Rule Instantly

If you see:

* Power with brackets → $(\dots)^n$
* trig with brackets → $\sin(\dots)$
* exponential with exponent → $e^{(\dots)}$
* log with argument → $\ln(\dots)$

Then inner function exists → chain rule needed.

---

## General Chain Rule — Composite of **n Functions**

When many functions are stacked inside each other, you just keep applying the same idea repeatedly.

---

### General Composite Form

Suppose you have:

$$
y = f_1\big(f_2\big(f_3(\dots f_n(x)\dots)\big)\big)
$$

This means:

* $f_n(x)$ is the **innermost**
* $f_1(\cdot)$ is the **outermost**

---

### General Derivative Formula

The derivative is the **product of derivatives of every layer**, evaluated step by step:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}
=

f_1'(f_2(\dots))
\cdot
f_2'(f_3(\dots))
\cdot
f_3'(\dots)
\cdots
f_n'(x)
$$

👉 Differentiate each layer\
👉 Multiply all derivatives together\
👉 Move from outside → inside.

---

### Intuition (Why Product Appears)

Each layer transforms the input:

* (x) changes → affects inner function
* that change affects next layer
* and so on…

Total rate of change = **all individual change rates multiplied**.

Think of it like a pipeline of transformations.

---

## Step-by-Step Mechanical Method

When you see a scary nested function:

### Step 1 — Identify layers

Example:

$$
y=\sin\left((x^2+1)^3\right)
$$

Layers:

1. outer: $\sin(\cdot)$
2. middle: power $u^3$
3. inner: $x^2+1$

---

### Step 2 — Differentiate layer by layer

* derivative of sin → cos
* derivative of $u^3$ → $3u^2$
* derivative of $x^2+1$ → $2x$

---

### Step 3 — Multiply everything

$$
y'=\cos((x^2+1)^3)\cdot 3(x^2+1)^2\cdot 2x
$$

That’s literally the general rule in action.

---

### Compact Notation

Let:

$$
y=f_1(u_1),\quad
u_1=f_2(u_2),\quad
\dots,\quad
u_{n-1}=f_n(x)
$$

Then:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx}
=

\frac{dy}{du_1}
\cdot
\frac{du_1}{du_2}
\cdot
\dots
\cdot
\frac{du_{n-1}}{dx}
$$

You’ll see this exact structure later in **backpropagation** in ML.

---

### Quick Recognition Trick

If you can keep putting brackets inside brackets:

* $sin(exp(ln(x²+1)))$ 
* $e^{\sqrt{\sin(x^3)}}$

you are stacking chain rules.

Count layers → multiply that many derivatives.
